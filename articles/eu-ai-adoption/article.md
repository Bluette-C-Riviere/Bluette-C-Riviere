 # The EU's AI Adoption Gap: Why Wider Access Does Not Yet Translate into Deeper Business Integration

**Category:** Strategic Perspectives & Industry Commentary  
**Topic:** European AI adoption, digital transformation and organisational maturity  
**Geographical scope:** European Union  
**Primary source:** European Commission Joint Research Centre, 2026  
**Target audience:** Business leaders, technology providers, compliance professionals, digital transformation leaders and policymakers

---

## Executive perspective

Artificial intelligence adoption across the European Union is accelerating, but increased access to AI technologies does not necessarily indicate that businesses are integrating them into their core operations.

The European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC), in its 2026 report *Measuring AI Adoption in the European Union: Methodology and Results for Strategic Sectors*, identifies a significant distinction between the growing use of accessible, general-purpose AI tools and the more demanding process of integrating AI into production, research, logistics and other operational activities.

Between 2021 and 2025, the proportion of EU enterprises using at least one AI technology increased from 8% to 20%. However, much of this growth has been driven by technologies supporting text analysis, content generation and other relatively accessible applications, rather than deeper operational integration.

The distinction matters. Adopting an AI tool is not equivalent to transforming a business process. Meaningful integration requires more than access to technology: it depends on digital infrastructure, relevant expertise, data availability, organisational capabilities and an appropriate governance framework.

For European businesses, the next stage of AI adoption will therefore be less about the availability of tools and more about the ability to integrate them effectively, responsibly and sustainably.

## 1. AI adoption is increasing, but the nature of adoption matters

The European Union has positioned artificial intelligence as an important component of its competitiveness, industrial transformation and digital policy agenda.

The European Commission's Apply AI Strategy reflects this ambition by encouraging the integration of AI into strategic sectors and business activities. This represents a shift towards a more sector-specific approach, recognising that the opportunities and constraints associated with AI differ across industries.

The JRC's 2026 analysis provides an indication of how adoption is developing.

Across the EU economy, the proportion of enterprises using at least one AI technology increased from 8% in 2021 to 20% in 2025.

However, adoption remains uneven across strategic sectors.

Media, energy and electronic communications are among the sectors with the highest adoption levels, while mobility, transport and automotive, tourism and agri-food remain considerably lower.

In 2025, media recorded the highest adoption rate, at 38%.

These differences demonstrate that AI adoption cannot be understood through a single European indicator. Industry characteristics, available technologies and the suitability of AI applications for specific business processes all influence the pace and nature of adoption.

More importantly, the overall adoption rate does not explain how deeply AI is embedded within an organisation.

A business using AI to generate marketing content and another using machine learning to support production processes may both be classified as AI adopters. Yet the operational implications, organisational requirements and potential business impact are fundamentally different.

This distinction is central to understanding the current European adoption landscape.

## 2. The adoption gap: From accessible tools to operational integration

One of the most significant findings in the JRC report is that the recent increase in AI adoption has been driven largely by accessible, general-purpose technologies.

These include systems capable of analysing or generating text, programming code, images, audio and video.

Their accessibility has lowered the barriers to experimentation. Businesses can introduce AI-assisted activities without necessarily undertaking substantial changes to their existing technology environments or operational structures.

The report identifies particularly strong adoption of AI technologies for analysing written language, generating content and converting spoken language into machine-readable formats.

By contrast, technologies requiring deeper operational integration, including workflow automation and advanced data analysis, remain less widely adopted.

A similar pattern appears when examining the purposes for which enterprises use AI.

Marketing, sales and administration are among the predominant applications across sectors. Adoption in production processes, logistics, research and development and ICT security is less widespread in several industries.

The difference is not simply technological.

General-purpose tools can often be introduced into existing activities with relatively limited organisational disruption. Integrating AI into core processes is considerably more demanding.

It may require:

- Changes to existing business processes and operating models
- Integration with enterprise applications and existing IT infrastructure
- Access to reliable, structured and relevant data
- Clear allocation of responsibilities and decision-making authority
- Appropriate human oversight and control mechanisms
- Technical expertise to evaluate, maintain and monitor AI systems

The distinction is important for business leaders.

An organisation can demonstrate increasing AI usage without necessarily achieving a comparable level of operational transformation.

The challenge is therefore not simply to increase the number of employees or departments using AI. It is to identify where AI can create measurable operational value and establish the conditions necessary for its effective integration.

## 3. Sectoral differences reveal the importance of technological and organisational maturity

The JRC findings demonstrate that AI adoption is strongly influenced by the relationship between available technologies and the operational characteristics of individual sectors.

Media, energy and electronic communications illustrate more diversified adoption patterns, with AI being used across a broader range of business functions.

In the media sector, AI applications are particularly aligned with activities involving text, images, audio and video. This technological compatibility helps explain why the sector has experienced substantial growth in AI adoption.

Energy presents a different profile, with more diversified applications, including workflow automation, decision-support systems and machine-learning-based data analysis.

Other sectors demonstrate more concentrated adoption.

In tourism and agri-food, for example, the growth of accessible AI technologies has translated primarily into increased use for marketing, sales and administration, rather than extensive integration into production activities.

These differences suggest that the potential value of AI cannot be assessed independently of the business environment in which it is deployed.

The same technology may offer considerable value in one industry while requiring substantial adaptation or presenting different operational constraints in another.

This has implications for organisations evaluating their AI strategies.

A generic adoption roadmap may provide a starting point, but it cannot replace an assessment of sector-specific processes, technological requirements and operational maturity.

Businesses should therefore evaluate AI opportunities in relation to their actual activities rather than treating adoption as a standalone digitalisation objective.

## 4. Digital maturity is a prerequisite for deeper AI integration

The relationship between AI adoption and broader digitalisation is another important finding.

The JRC analysis indicates that enterprises with higher levels of digitalisation have experienced both higher AI adoption and faster growth in adoption.

The report also identifies a relationship between deeper AI use in production and organisational structures adapted to support AI integration.

This suggests that AI adoption is not an isolated technological development.

It is connected to the broader capabilities of an organisation, including its digital infrastructure, management practices, workforce expertise and ability to adapt existing processes.

An enterprise with established digital systems, structured data environments and appropriate technical capabilities is generally better positioned to integrate AI into its operations than one still addressing fundamental digitalisation challenges.

This relationship raises an important strategic consideration.

Investing in AI without addressing underlying organisational and technological weaknesses may result in isolated applications rather than sustainable operational improvements.

For organisations, the sequence of transformation matters.

AI initiatives should be evaluated alongside existing digital capabilities, data governance arrangements, process maturity and internal expertise.

The objective should not be to introduce AI wherever technically possible, but to establish whether the organisation has the necessary foundations to use it effectively.

## 5. Expertise and regulatory uncertainty remain significant barriers

Technology availability alone does not resolve the challenges associated with adoption.

The JRC identifies a lack of relevant expertise as the principal barrier to wider AI uptake.

Approximately 70% of enterprises that had considered adopting AI but had not done so cited insufficient expertise as an obstacle.

The report also identifies a growing relevance of regulatory concerns between 2023 and 2025, particularly in relation to data protection, privacy and the legal consequences of deploying AI systems.

This is significant because the barriers to AI adoption are evolving.

Concerns relating to cost, data availability and quality, and incompatibility with existing IT systems have become less prominent. Meanwhile, the shortage of expertise and regulatory uncertainty remain important constraints.

For businesses, this creates a broader capability challenge.

Effective AI integration requires professionals who can understand the technology, assess its suitability for particular activities and evaluate the associated operational and regulatory implications.

It also requires organisations to establish appropriate governance arrangements.

Depending on the intended use, these may involve:

- Identifying and documenting AI use cases
- Assessing the purpose and operational context of AI systems
- Understanding data sources and data-processing activities
- Defining responsibilities between business, IT and compliance functions
- Establishing appropriate oversight and monitoring arrangements
- Assessing relevant legal and regulatory requirements
- Maintaining documentation supporting internal governance and accountability

These considerations should not be interpreted as a universal list of statutory obligations. Their applicability depends on the systems involved, their intended purposes and the regulatory framework governing their deployment.

Nevertheless, they illustrate why AI adoption increasingly requires coordination between technology, business operations, risk management and compliance functions.

## 6. The governance challenge: Connecting AI adoption with business accountability

As AI becomes more widely accessible, organisations face a growing need to distinguish experimentation from controlled operational deployment.

The use of a general-purpose AI tool does not automatically imply that an organisation has established an appropriate framework for managing its use.

Equally, a system integrated into a critical business process raises different considerations from an employee using AI to support routine administrative activities.

The relevant governance approach should therefore reflect the context and nature of the deployment.

Organisations need to understand where AI is being used, what business activities it supports, which data it processes and who is responsible for its operation and oversight.

This becomes particularly important when AI systems interact with existing enterprise applications, customer information, financial processes or regulated activities.

The European regulatory environment adds another dimension.

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act establishes a risk-based regulatory framework, while existing legislation concerning data protection and other regulated activities may also remain relevant depending on the deployment.

However, regulatory compliance should not be approached as a separate exercise conducted after the technology has been implemented.

Governance considerations are more effective when incorporated into the assessment and design of AI use cases from the outset.

This includes identifying relevant regulatory requirements, defining responsibilities, evaluating risks and determining what evidence is needed to demonstrate appropriate oversight.

For organisations, the objective is to establish a proportionate governance framework that supports innovation while maintaining accountability and control.

## 7. What European businesses should consider next

The findings of the JRC report point towards a transition from widespread experimentation to more selective and operationally integrated AI adoption.

For organisations seeking to move in this direction, several considerations deserve attention.

**1. Assess existing digital maturity**

Evaluate the organisation's current digital infrastructure, data environment, system interoperability and process maturity before prioritising AI investments.

**2. Identify business-relevant use cases**

Prioritise applications based on their operational relevance, feasibility and expected contribution to business objectives rather than the availability or popularity of particular AI tools.

**3. Distinguish experimentation from production deployment**

Establish clear criteria for moving AI initiatives from exploratory use into operational environments, including appropriate testing, validation, oversight and monitoring.

**4. Develop internal expertise**

Address the skills gap through targeted training and the development of capabilities across business, technology, risk and compliance functions.

**5. Integrate governance into implementation**

Identify applicable regulatory requirements and establish responsibilities, documentation and control arrangements appropriate to each use case.

**6. Measure operational outcomes**

Move beyond measuring the number of employees using AI or the number of deployed tools. Evaluate whether AI is contributing to improvements in productivity, process efficiency, quality or other relevant business outcomes.

These considerations provide a starting point for organisations seeking to move from isolated AI experimentation towards more structured and sustainable integration.

## 8. The next phase of European AI adoption

The JRC report provides evidence of substantial growth in AI adoption across European enterprises. However, it also demonstrates that adoption levels alone provide an incomplete picture of technological transformation.

The next phase will depend increasingly on the ability of organisations to integrate AI into business processes, develop the necessary internal expertise and establish appropriate governance arrangements.

This transition is unlikely to occur uniformly across sectors.

The suitability of available technologies, existing digital capabilities and the organisational requirements of individual industries will continue to influence the pace and depth of adoption.

For European businesses, the strategic question is therefore changing.

It is no longer sufficient to ask whether an organisation is using artificial intelligence. The more relevant question is whether it has the technological, organisational and governance capabilities required to derive sustainable value from it.

**AI adoption is becoming less a question of access to technology and more a question of organisational readiness.**

The distinction between these two dimensions will increasingly determine which organisations move beyond experimentation and achieve meaningful operational integration.
