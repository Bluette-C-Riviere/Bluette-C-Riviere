# The Revised Schengen Borders Code: Duration Limits, Proportionality and Regulatory Oversight

## Executive summary

The reintroduction of internal border controls within the Schengen Area remains legally possible, but the regulatory framework governing their use has become more demanding.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1717 amended the Schengen Borders Code (SBC), Regulation (EU) 2016/399, introducing revised duration limits, more structured notification requirements, stronger proportionality assessments and additional European Commission oversight.

The revision does not abolish Member States' discretion to temporarily reinstate internal border controls. Instead, it reinforces the exceptional nature of such measures and establishes additional safeguards intended to prevent their indefinite or insufficiently justified continuation.

A European Parliament briefing, *The Economic Impact of Internal Border Controls* (PE 790.632, 2026), provides useful context for understanding how the revised framework operates in practice. It documents the growing recurrence of internal border controls and examines their economic and territorial consequences.

For regulatory and compliance professionals, three developments deserve particular attention:

- The introduction of more structured duration limits, including a maximum of three years in major exceptional situations
- The explicit requirement to consider the effects of controls on free movement and cross-border regions
- The strengthened consultation, notification and oversight mechanisms involving the European Commission and affected Member States

The central regulatory question is no longer simply whether a Member State can reintroduce internal border controls. It is whether their necessity, proportionality, duration and implementation can be adequately justified.

## 1. Regulatory background: the Schengen Borders Code

The Schengen Area is founded on the principle that internal borders should remain free from routine border controls on persons.

Article 22 of Regulation (EU) 2016/399 establishes the general rule that internal borders may be crossed at any point without a border check on persons, irrespective of nationality.

This principle forms part of the wider European framework for free movement.

Article 26(2) TFEU defines the internal market as an area without internal frontiers in which the free movement of goods, persons, services and capital is ensured.

However, the absence of routine internal border controls does not eliminate the possibility of exceptional intervention.

Article 25 of the Schengen Borders Code allows Member States to temporarily reintroduce controls where a serious threat to public policy or internal security exists.

Such measures must remain exceptional, necessary and proportionate.

The 2024 revision was intended to clarify and strengthen the framework governing these derogations while preserving the functioning of the Schengen Area.

## 2. What changed under Regulation (EU) 2024/1717?

The revision introduced several important changes to the Schengen Borders Code.

### 2.1. More structured categories of serious threats

The revised Article 25 clarifies the circumstances that may constitute a serious threat to public policy or internal security.

These include:

- Terrorist incidents and threats, including serious organised crime
- Large-scale public health emergencies
- Sudden large-scale unauthorised movements of third-country nationals between Member States
- Large-scale or high-profile international events

The identification of these categories provides a more structured framework for assessing the grounds on which internal border controls may be introduced.

However, the existence of a qualifying threat does not automatically establish that border controls are necessary or proportionate.

Member States must still demonstrate why the measure is justified in the circumstances concerned.

### 2.2. Revised duration limits

One of the most significant changes concerns the maximum duration of temporary internal border controls.

Article 25a distinguishes between different types of threats.

| Situation | Initial duration | Maximum duration |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unforeseeable serious threat | Up to 1 month | 3 months in total |
| Foreseeable serious threat | Up to 6 months | 2 years |
| Major exceptional situation beyond 2 years | Additional exceptional prolongations | 3 years in total |

The possibility of extending controls beyond two years is subject to additional safeguards, including risk assessments and European Commission involvement.

The distinction between foreseeable and unforeseeable threats is important because it determines the applicable notification, duration and justification requirements.

Duration limits should not be interpreted as an automatic entitlement to maintain controls for the entire permitted period.

Each prolongation remains subject to the applicable necessity and proportionality requirements.

### 2.3. A more explicit proportionality assessment

Article 26 strengthens the assessment required before internal border controls are introduced or prolonged.

Member States must consider whether the measure is necessary and proportionate to the identified threat.

Importantly, the assessment must also address:

- The impact on free movement
- The consequences for cross-border regions
- The availability and suitability of alternative measures
- The possibility of mitigating negative effects

This is a significant development because it explicitly recognises the economic and social interdependence of regions located on opposite sides of internal borders.

The assessment is therefore not limited to the security interests of the Member State introducing controls.

It must also consider the wider consequences for the Schengen Area.

### 2.4. Stronger notification and reporting requirements

The revised framework also strengthens procedural transparency.

Article 27 establishes the information that Member States must provide when notifying the reintroduction or prolongation of internal border controls.

This includes the identified threat, the affected border sections, the intended duration and the assessment of necessity and proportionality.

Article 27a introduces a more structured consultation process involving the European Commission, the Member States concerned and relevant EU agencies.

The Commission may issue an opinion where it has concerns about necessity or proportionality.

Where controls reach 12 months in total, the Commission must issue an opinion under the conditions established by the revised provision.

These mechanisms strengthen the institutional scrutiny of prolonged controls without transferring the initial decision to reinstate them entirely to the European Commission.

## 3. What remains unchanged?

The revision should not be interpreted as a prohibition on internal border controls.

Several fundamental elements remain in place.

**Member State discretion**

Member States retain the ability to initiate temporary internal border controls under Article 25 where the applicable conditions are met.

**Free movement remains the default**

The general principle established by Article 22 remains unchanged.

**Security derogations remain possible**

The Treaties and the Schengen Borders Code continue to provide a framework for exceptional security measures.

**Alternative measures remain important**

Article 23 preserves the possibility of certain police checks within national territories, provided that they do not have an effect equivalent to border checks.

The distinction between systematic border controls and other legitimate police activities remains particularly relevant.

## 4. Regulatory developments in practice: European Commission oversight

The effectiveness of the revised framework depends not only on its legal provisions but also on how they are applied.

On 2 June 2026, the European Commission issued opinions concerning the temporary reintroduction of internal border controls by nine Schengen states:

Austria, Denmark, France, Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Norway, Slovenia and Sweden.

The opinions assessed necessity, proportionality, alternative measures and the mitigation of negative consequences for cross-border mobility.

The Commission also included recommendations intended to enable Member States to work towards gradually lifting internal border controls.

This development illustrates the practical importance of the revised Article 27a mechanism.

The Commission's role is not limited to receiving notifications. It can assess the justification for prolonged controls and encourage Member States to consider alternative arrangements.

The 2026 State of Schengen Report also emphasises operational cooperation, information exchange and non-systematic risk-based checks as alternatives that can help preserve free movement.

These developments demonstrate a stronger focus on the practical application of the revised framework.

## 5. Judicial scrutiny and the requirement for a sufficiently justified threat

The regulatory framework must also be considered alongside the case law of the Court of Justice of the European Union.

In Case C-368/20, *NW v Landespolizeidirektion Steiermark*, the Court examined the conditions under which internal border controls could be prolonged.

The judgment reinforces the exceptional nature of derogations from the principle of free movement and the need for Member States to respect the applicable legal conditions.

The wider implication is that the repeated extension of border controls cannot be treated as a purely administrative exercise.

The justification must remain connected to the actual circumstances and the applicable legal requirements.

For Member States, this places greater importance on the quality of risk assessments, supporting evidence and the examination of alternative measures.

## 6. Economic consequences as part of the proportionality assessment

The revised framework gives greater visibility to the impact of internal border controls on cross-border regions.

This is particularly relevant given the findings of the European Parliament briefing *The Economic Impact of Internal Border Controls*.

The briefing identifies several areas affected by recurring controls:

- Transport and logistics operations
- Cross-border employment
- Regional trade and business activity
- Mobility and tourism
- Social and territorial cohesion

It also highlights an important limitation: reliable, structured empirical monitoring of the aggregate economic consequences remains insufficient.

The available estimates are drawn from different studies, sectoral reports and regional examples. They cannot automatically be applied to every Member State or border crossing.

Nevertheless, the evidence demonstrates why the economic and territorial effects of controls cannot be excluded from the proportionality assessment.

The revised framework therefore creates a stronger legal basis for considering consequences that extend beyond the immediate security objective.

## 7. Practical considerations for regulatory monitoring

For organisations and professionals following European regulatory developments, several areas deserve continued monitoring.

| Monitoring area | Regulatory relevance |
|---|---|
| National notifications | Identification of newly introduced or prolonged controls |
| Article 25a duration limits | Assessment of applicable legal timeframes |
| Article 27 notifications | Transparency and supporting justification |
| Article 27a opinions | European Commission scrutiny |
| National court decisions | Interpretation of necessity and proportionality |
| Alternative measures | Developments in police and operational cooperation |
| Economic impact studies | Evidence supporting proportionality assessments |

These are proposed regulatory-monitoring priorities rather than obligations imposed on private organisations by the Schengen Borders Code.

For businesses operating across Schengen borders, monitoring may also help identify geographical exposure to potential transport and workforce disruptions.

## 8. Regulatory outlook

The revised Schengen Borders Code represents an effort to reconcile national security requirements with the preservation of free movement.

Its effectiveness will depend on several developments.

First, Member States must demonstrate that internal border controls remain necessary and proportionate rather than relying on repeated notifications without sufficiently specific justification.

Second, the European Commission must continue applying its consultation and oversight mechanisms consistently.

Third, alternative operational arrangements must be sufficiently developed to provide credible responses to security concerns without unnecessary disruption to internal mobility.

Finally, the economic consequences of controls require better empirical measurement.

The 2026 European Parliament briefing identifies the absence of structured data as a major limitation in assessing the aggregate impact of internal border controls.

Improved monitoring would strengthen both policy evaluation and the evidence available for proportionality assessments.

## Conclusion

Regulation (EU) 2024/1717 does not eliminate the possibility of reintroducing internal border controls. It establishes a more demanding framework for their introduction, continuation and justification.

The revised duration limits, proportionality requirements, impact assessments and Commission oversight mechanisms reinforce the exceptional character of these measures.

The challenge now lies in implementation.

A regulatory framework can establish procedural safeguards, but its effectiveness ultimately depends on the quality of national assessments, the consistency of institutional scrutiny and the availability of proportionate alternatives.

For the Schengen Area, preserving free movement requires more than maintaining the formal principle of open internal borders. It requires ensuring that exceptional restrictions remain genuinely exceptional.
